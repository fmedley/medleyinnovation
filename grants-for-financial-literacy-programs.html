How Nonprofits Fund Financial Literacy Programs: 5 Grant and Funding Sources

By Medley Innovation · Published November 16, 2026

The short answer: most nonprofits do not win a grant labeled "financial literacy." They fund it as one part of a larger program, through workforce (WIOA), anti-poverty (CSBG), bank community reinvestment (CRA), housing counseling (HUD), or reentry (Second Chance Act) dollars. For each source, the first question is who holds the money and how you reach them.

The five sources at a glance

1. WIOA workforce funding

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act funds local workforce boards, which in turn fund youth and adult employment programs. Financial literacy education is one of the 14 program elements youth programs must make available, and financial literacy services are an individualized career service for adults.

WIOA money does not usually flow to a nonprofit as a stand-alone financial literacy grant. Nonprofits reach it by becoming a provider or partner of their local workforce board. Our guide to WIOA financial literacy requirements explains what the rules ask for.

2. Community Services Block Grant (CSBG)

CSBG is a federal anti-poverty block grant run by the Office of Community Services at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. States must pass through at least 90 percent of their award to local entities such as Community Action Agencies.

Those local entities choose services based on a community needs assessment. The federal CSBG fact sheet names financial literacy education among its examples, alongside employment, education, and income and asset building services. If you are not a Community Action Agency, the route in is a partnership with the one that serves your area.

3. Bank support under the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA)

The CRA encourages banks to help meet the credit needs of the communities where they operate, including low- and moderate-income neighborhoods. According to the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's financial capability fact sheet, a bank's financial literacy and financial education activities may be eligible for positive CRA consideration.

That gives banks a reason to sponsor a program, lend staff as volunteers, or co-deliver workshops with a nonprofit. This is a relationship, not an application: approach the community development or CRA officer at banks with branches in your area, and be ready to describe who you serve.

4. HUD housing counseling grants

HUD's Comprehensive Housing Counseling grants pay for housing counseling and group education. Only HUD-approved housing counseling agencies, intermediaries, and qualifying state housing finance agencies can apply directly. According to HUD's grant FAQs, an agency without HUD approval can affiliate with an approved intermediary or state housing finance agency to access subgrant funding.

This source fits when your financial education is tied to housing: preparing to buy a home, renting, or avoiding foreclosure.

5. Second Chance Act reentry grants

The Second Chance Act Community-based Reentry Program, run by the Bureau of Justice Assistance, provides funding and technical assistance to nonprofit organizations and tribal governments that serve people leaving incarceration. The services it lists include educational, literacy, and vocational services, mentoring, and help securing housing.

The program page does not name financial literacy specifically, so read the current funding notice to see how financial education fits within the services it allows. Our article on what a reentry financial literacy curriculum should cover can help you describe that component.

Also worth pursuing: foundations and local government

Community foundations, private foundations, and city and county programs fund financial education too. They are often more flexible, and can be a good match for a pilot that later grows into one of the sources above.

What makes a program fundable

  • A defined population. Funders want to know exactly who you serve and why they need it.
  • A written curriculum. Show the topics, the number of sessions, and how the material fits your participants.
  • Outcome data. Pre- and post-assessments and attendance records turn "we ran workshops" into a result a funder can report.
  • A qualified instructor. Name who teaches, whether that is your staff or a provider.
  • A fit with the funder's purpose. Describe the program in the funder's terms: employment for WIOA, self-sufficiency for CSBG, housing for HUD.
